\title{Loki~III: Reverse Engineering Structural Netlists without a Language Model\\
How far do provenance, a Bayesian L1 and a graph L2 get on their own?}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Peter Jamieson}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Electrical and Computer Engineering, Miami University\\
Oxford, OH, USA\\
jamiespa@miamioh.edu}}

\begin{abstract}
Gate-Breaker showed that a language model can recover functional RTL from a
gate-level netlist, and it ended by naming the graph techniques its model
needed: clustering to name buses, a classification of signals as datapath or
control, and partitioning. Loki~III asks the complementary question: how much
structure can be recovered \emph{without} a language model, from provenance, a
Bayesian hypothesis flow and graph analysis alone? This paper describes the
benchmarks built to answer that question and reports the recovery statistics
under the Gate-Breaker metrics. The benchmarks are 68 synthesizable micro
designs compiled by a provenance-tracked front end into named, labelled,
anonymised netlist datasets; a renamed-instance protocol then proves the
pipeline naming-neutral across 105,646 renamed identifiers. On the L1
hypothesis flow, the structures that are already reverse engineered by the
synthesis flow recover almost perfectly (hard adders 67 of 67, hard
multipliers 30 of 30), while the structures the flow erased recover weakly
(macro-F1 0.354 on the first floor, 0.108 on the second, calibrated). The L2
graph stage beats a 1-WL baseline on real micros once node, graphlet, WL and
NPN features combine (macro-F1 0.4241 against 0.2717) and its clusters carry
the designer's hierarchy (Louvain modularity 0.4220). We state the honest
limits: no language model step exists, the recovered structure is structural
rather than functional, and the paper's own determinism defects are reported.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Gate-Breaker~\cite{faruque2025gatebreaker} takes a gate-level netlist with obfuscated wire names,
formats it into a four-step prompt (interface, decomposition, function, generation), asks a
language model to write RTL, and checks the result by simulation. On 156 VerilogEval
problems~\cite{liu2023verilogeval} its best model recovered functional RTL for 75.1\% of the
runs, and 53.4\% on the hardest quartile. The paper's own conclusion is the interesting part:
external graph tools should help the model -- clustering to name buses, a classification of
signals as datapath or control, and partitioning of the netlist into smaller parts.

Loki~III (\url{https://github.com/drpaj12/loki}) is the Yggdrasil~III suite's reverse-engineering
tool, and it targets exactly those three items -- with no language model step at all. Its
pipeline is L0, a provenance ground-truth dataset made from the benchmarks; L1, a Bayesian
hypothesis flow whose pattern detectors propose structures and whose proofs confirm them; and
L2, graph analysis (clustering, structural features, graph neural networks) over the same
netlists. The research question this paper answers is: \emph{how good are the L0, L1 and L2
experiments, without more intelligence, at recovering structure?} The answer matters for two
audiences. For the Gate-Breaker line, it prices the graph side of the future work the paper
names. For reverse engineering, it measures what a provenance-tracked front end and honest
statistical machinery can do before any model is added.

The contributions are: (1) a benchmark method -- provenance-tracked, labelled, anonymised
netlist datasets with a renamed-instance protocol that proves naming neutrality, so the
pipeline can be evaluated on designs whose names carry nothing; (2) the L1 and L2 recovery
statistics under the Gate-Breaker metrics and under Loki's own (precision, recall, calibration,
clustering quality, GNN against a 1-WL baseline); (3) the honest limits, including two
determinism defects the experiments found.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{The provenance problem and Odin III}

Synthesis modifies the designer's functional intention: flattening removes hierarchy,
optimization merges operators, and mapping covers logic with cells that carry no source names.
Linking each netlist object back to the HDL that produced it is the provenance
problem~\cite{gharibian2010finding}. Odin~III, the provenance-tracked front end under Loki,
keeps that link: every object of its one IR records its source location, and the link
survives elaboration, optimization, partial mapping and the linked ABC flow. Loki stands on
that link: its L0 datasets carry the answer key the synthesis tool kept, which is what makes
the recovery statistics in this paper possible at all.

\subsection{Gate-Breaker as the method baseline}

Gate-Breaker measures recovery by functional correctness and by the similarity of the
recovered and reference RTL: Success Rate (the fraction of $K$ samples that simulate
correctly), Success@$k$ (the fraction of designs solved within the first $k$ samples), AST
similarity (mean subtree Jaccard over normalized identifiers, depth $d=3$) and CFG similarity
(a normalized shortest-path node kernel over control-flow graphs). Its complexity score
weights sequential blocks, generates, loops, instantiations, always blocks, buses,
multiplexers, cases, arithmetic operators, assignments and concatenations into quartiles Q1
(simple combinational) through Q4 (complex sequential). Loki reports its own statistics on
the same axes where they apply -- recovery rate per structure kind, calibration error,
clustering quality against the known hierarchy, and GNN accuracy against a 1-WL baseline --
and the Gate-Breaker metrics on the replication runs.

\section{Methodology: the benchmarks and the pipeline}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{L0: provenance ground-truth datasets}
\label{sec:l0}

The benchmark set is the 68-design micro suite (counters, registers, arithmetic, ALUs, muxes,
comparators, small FSMs) that Odin~III already compiles and checks against Yosys. L0 compiles
each design on the k6 and EArch architectures and on the LPM and cmos6 libraries into floors:
\code{f1} (the mapped VTR netlist), \code{f2} (the LPM library netlist) and \code{f3} (the
cmos6 gate netlist). Every netlist object carries its provenance (source file and line), a
neutral \code{n}$k$ name from one shared counter, and a label: the structure kind
(\code{construct\_type}) is the answer key in \code{labels.csv}, and the netlist-visible
hard-block kind (\code{cell\_type}) travels in the graph labels, so structures that are
already reverse engineered stay visible to the reconstruction. \code{loki check} validates
every dataset: 68 designs, 269 floors, 100\% labelled rows, zero errors. The two hard-block
designs the v1.0 front end refused now compile on the v1.1.0 SystemVerilog front end, so the
set is complete.

\emph{Renamed instances.} A separate protocol asks the naming question directly: rename every
identifier of every netlist (105,646 identifiers across 269 netlists) through a shared
permutation, run the pipeline on the renamed floors, and inverse-map every token back. The
verifier reports token-for-token equality with the original, zero failures, and the base and
renamed score digests are byte-identical (sha256 over all run scores, 204 of 204 pairs) --
the pipeline is naming-neutral, so nothing it recovers is an artefact of a name.

\subsection{L1: the Bayesian hypothesis flow}
\label{sec:l1}

L1 is a round-based hypothesis flow over the netlist, with no language model. Pattern
detectors (arith, bus, ff\_pattern, fsm, mux, role, seqfn, unit, and the \code{hard} detector
of Task~17) propose structures with likelihoods; \code{cone\_check} proofs confirm them by
equivalence against the lowered cone; Bayes' rule updates each hypothesis from the evidence;
and a confirmed guess commits as a new level of an ordinary Odin~III design
(\code{raise}, \code{submod}), so the result can be written, drawn and traced back to the
gates. The \code{hard} detector reads the \code{unmap} pass's facts: a hard adder, multiplier
or memory that the flow already reverse engineered is recovered directly, with the
decline reasons the pass recorded (carry-in chains and the like) kept as open hypotheses. A
committed calibration (per-floor weights over the structural, WL and NPN features) turns
likelihoods into probabilities; the reported expected calibration error (ECE) is the honest
check on that step.

\subsection{L2: graph analysis}
\label{sec:l2}

L2 reads the same datasets as graphs: node features from Weisfeiler-Leman colours, NPN
signatures and ORCA graphlet counts; region and partition records; four clustering methods
(k-means, affinity propagation, spectral, Louvain) scored by modularity and by normalized
mutual information against the known module hierarchy; and a graph neural network (GraphSAGE,
and GCN as the variant) trained on the labelled graphs with a 1-WL baseline as the honest
competitor. The GNN targets the construct type of every node; the question it answers is
whether the learned node features beat what one Weisfeiler-Leman round already knows.

\subsection{f3: recovery from the cmos6 gate netlist}
\label{sec:f3-results}

Gate-Breaker's own input is a cmos6 gate netlist, so we built the f3 floor (65 of 68 micro
designs; the gate reader refuses three hard-block sources) and asked the same questions of
it. The L0 answer key is complete at this level: all 50,047 gate nodes carry a label row, a
cmos6 \code{cell\_type} and an HDL span. What provenance reconstructs of the reference is
nearly complete but not total: 112 module scopes against 113 module definitions (99.1\%; 54
of 65 designs complete) and 611 distinct leaf signal names against 666 declared signals
(91.7\%; median design 100\%, minimum 55.6\%). The module gap is reference-side (generate
branches and unused submodules never elaborate); the signal gap is synthesis-side (nets that
mapping created or merged have no original name left). The ceiling is consequently what the
mapping preserved, not the source RTL.

Table~\ref{tab:f3-l1} reports L1 on the 15 f3 test designs (23,098 gate nodes), base and
renamed, with a calibration fitted on the 50 train designs' f3 floors. The result is the
strict one: no construct is counted (0 of 144 ground-truth instances; macro-F1 0.0) and no
hypothesis is committed at all. The f3 priors are strongly negative because the gate-level
detectors are imprecise on the train floors (comparator $-9.24$ from 31,036 proposals with
2 true), so the best calibrated confidence in any test design is a bus at $p=0.764$, below
the 0.9 commit threshold, and the L1-stop Verilog is byte-identical to the input netlist in
15 of 15 designs. Without calibration the detectors count 1,166 constructs for 2 matches
(macro-F1 0.038; one adder, one register) and 2,835 buses for 0 matches. Buses are proposed
but never reconstructed: 2,569 counted bus hypotheses against 120 ground-truth buses, 0
matched, so no width or bit order is recovered. The role detector labels every logic cell
datapath, which is 100\% correct only because the f3 answer key holds no control cells (0 of
50,047; f1 has 4): control-versus-data is not answerable from f3 provenance, and the
datapath F1 of 1.000 over 20,541 cells is vacuous. The hard detector stays f1-only by design
(\code{unmap} runs only on f1 floors with hard cells), so the 28 hard ground-truth instances
are unreachable here. Base and renamed \code{score.json} digests are identical
(9368cfc26303): naming contributes no decision bit at f3 either.

The AST and CFG deltas (Table~\ref{tab:f3-sim}) follow from the zero-commit result: with the
f3 calibration L1-stop is byte-identical to L0-stop, so both metrics have delta exactly
0.0000 in every quartile (AST over 15 designs; CFG over the 13 whose CFG the probe can
finish, 12,000 nodes being its practical $O(n^2)$ limit). The uncalibrated diagnostic, where
L1 commits 1,535 hypotheses in 11 of 15 designs, moves the means by +0.0111 (AST) and
$-0.0075$ (CFG), with mixed per-quartile signs; the commits are mostly wrong \code{eq\_const}
and bus pieces, so this is not evidence about recovery quality. A flatten control (Odin~III
\code{proc; flatten}, which also lowers the committed processes) inlines the committed
hierarchy and raises both metrics over the gate netlist: AST 0.4576 against 0.4044
(+0.0533, all 15 designs) and CFG 0.6941 against 0.6382 (+0.0559, the 12 designs the probe
can score).
Algorithms~2 and~3 read a single module, while Loki's L1 commits a hierarchy, so a recovery
that produces hierarchy is systematically penalised until it is flattened: for a
netlist-to-RTL benchmark this is a metric limitation, not a property of the recovery.

\subsection{L2: clusters that carry the hierarchy, and a GNN that beats 1-WL}
\label{sec:l2-results}

Table~\ref{tab:l2} scores the four clusterings against the known module hierarchy. Louvain
leads (modularity 0.4220, NMI 0.1360); k-means and affinity propagation do not find the
hierarchy (negative modularity), which is itself a result: graph structure alone, without
the labels, favours the community methods. 271 of 272 partition records are identical
between base and renamed floors; the one difference exposed a determinism defect in the
spectral path (repeated runs on the same base design give modularities -0.0079, -0.0410 and
0.0252), reported as an open issue rather than hidden.

The GNN result answers the paper's question directly. A node-only GraphSAGE loses to the
1-WL baseline on real micros (macro-F1 0.2413 against 0.2711); combining node, graphlet, WL
and NPN features turns it around (0.3545 against 0.2711 on the earlier real-micro run), and
the full labelled run of this experiment gives test macro-F1 0.4241 against 0.2717
(accuracy 0.8863 against 0.8492, 3,932 test nodes across 13 designs). The features carry the
recovery, not the message passing alone.

\subsection{The Gate-Breaker metrics}
\label{sec:gb-results}

Gate-Breaker's metrics compare a recovered RTL against a reference: Success Rate,
Success@$k$, AST similarity and CFG similarity. Loki recovers structure, not a new RTL text,
so the direct comparison is the replication: the Gate-Breaker harness itself, run on the
simple benchmarks with the only language model available on the host, gave success rate
0.933 on Q1 and 0.829 on Q2, Success@10 1.000 in both quartiles, AST similarity 0.576 and
0.444, CFG similarity 1.000 and 0.736, with 169 of 174 extracted candidates functionally
correct -- a first replication on the fallback micro set, not the paper's VerilogEval set.

Table~\ref{tab:veval} with the AST and CFG kernels recomputed with common-width feature
vectors (the first run padded per graph and truncated in the zip, which the v1.1.0 CFG probe
found; those columns are not comparable to it).

Seventy-four of the 78 Q1+Q2 designs are usable (three excluded because their synthesized
netlists fail the official testbench self-check, one because its 241\,KB prompt exceeds the
OS argument limit); every scored design is solved by attempt 5. The deltas against the

Success@5 +0.125, Success@10 +0.111, AST -0.164, CFG +0.083. The Q1 gap is harness
no-output, not wrong RTL; the Q2 AST gap is truth-table and case references against the
model's boolean expressions. Two caveats: the host's only model path routes through a
gateway that aliases a DeepSeek backend as Claude Opus (so the model column is not
verifiably the paper's comparison class), and the CLI exposes none of the paper's sampling
flags ($T{=}0.85$, $p{=}0.95$). The CFG column uses the common-width node kernel (the
odin3 CFG probe); the AST column uses the paper's pyverilog Algorithm 2.

What the two lines of work measure is complementary. The language model recovers functional
behaviour from the gates and names nothing reliably; Loki recovers the structures the flow
preserved (hard blocks, the hierarchy, the signal roles its features see) and proves what it
claims. The Gate-Breaker future work -- bus naming, datapath/control classification,
partitioning -- is exactly L2's table, and its numbers are the baseline a model-assisted
Loki would have to beat.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Three observations follow. First, \emph{the answer depends on what the flow erased}. The
structures that survived synthesis recover almost perfectly without any intelligence beyond
reading the pass's own facts; the structures synthesis erased (the counter behind its
flip-flop, the mux behind its gates) recover weakly, and no amount of honest Bayesian
machinery changes that without better detectors or a model. Second, \emph{the graph features
carry the recovery}: the GNN only beats 1-WL when node, graphlet, WL and NPN features
combine, which is the same lesson as the clustering table (community methods find the
hierarchy, centroid methods do not). Third, \emph{the benchmarks made the limits visible}:
the renamed-instance protocol proved naming neutrality over 105,646 identifiers, and the
same protocol exposed a rename-script defect (library \code{.blackbox} model names were
renamed, which silently removed every hard proposal on renamed floors) and the spectral
determinism defect -- both fixed or reported, and both invisible without the protocol.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Loki~III asks how far netlist-to-RTL reverse engineering gets without a language model. On
the benchmarks of this paper the answer is: the already-reverse-engineered structures,
perfectly; the rest, weakly but measurably, with calibration that reports its own error, a
graph stage whose features beat 1-WL, and clusters that carry the designer's hierarchy. The
methodology -- provenance-tracked labelled datasets plus the renamed-instance protocol -- is
the part we would export to any future comparison, with or without a model.
